Yuying Jin / Experience / Gospel Arts Website

Case study

Wishes in. Workflows out.

An artist paints a scripture every week, and preachers pass each painting on over WeChat to readers across Southeast Asia and North America. They came with a phased plan for a website and left the technical questions to me. My part was to turn that plan into a system, and to keep it running well after each request was done.

Asking what they didn't know to ask

The first brief was already a phased plan, from a home page and artwork browsing to, later, a nonprofit and a shop. I answered with the questions each item hides: who may upload, what needs review before it goes public, which languages come first, who signs in.

I also checked what the site would run on. The paintings had no dates or scripture references attached, and browsing by theme or date can't work without them, so I asked the artist to gather them first. When it turned out that readers open the site on phones inside WeChat, many of them older, I designed for phones first.

A look made for these paintings

I was stuck on the interface for a week, until a visit to a Trinity church, where the stained-glass windows matched the artist's style. I wrote a prompt asking for the paintings to be shown the way a church shows its windows, and Claude generated the first gallery page, in deep blue-black and gold. The artist said the colors and style were exactly what they loved. I reworked it from there and carried the theme through the site: a church and its stained glass, with each painting as a spiritual window.

Choosing tools a non-technical team can live with

I compared ways to manage the content and proposed the one that was easiest for people who aren't developers, cheapest to run, and still met every need. The spreadsheet and Dropbox folder the artist put together fed an early prototype, enough to design the pages and test how the paintings looked. Dropbox limits API access and wasn't stable enough to serve a live site, so it stayed at that stage. The archive, hundreds of images and over 300 MB, moved into a content studio.

Turning a request into a workflow

Most requests arrive as a one-time task. I treat each as the first run of something that will happen again.

"I'd like to upload my own work."

A complete artwork management flow in the studio: drafts, previews, bulk changes with Undo, a scripture picker that catches typos, plus written guides for the team.

"Put a watermark on every painting."

A watermark step built into every future upload, with defaults the artist can adjust per piece. Originals stay private, outside the public site.

Rolling out

"Readers should be able to share what moved them."

Comments and sign-in designed together with staff roles, a review queue and a moderation word list, so the team can look after the space.

Designed

"Can it reach people in three languages?"

Content is written once in Traditional Chinese, converted to Simplified and proofread, and paired with English, with scripture fetched in each language.

Working with a non-technical client

  • I set the agenda. I wrote the project's working documents, from the collaboration checklist and content list to how the site will be run, and I follow up on what's still open.
  • The artist stays on the content. I make the development decisions myself, so the artist can spend their time on the paintings. When something needs their call, I bring it as a short list of questions or a report.
  • Big ideas get a reality check. The artist has many big ideas. I weigh each one's priority, what it would need, and the time it would cost, so they can see when an idea needs more thought and can wait.
  • Plans before code. Bigger steps, like accounts and a shop, start as a written plan before anything is built.
