Yuying Jin / Experience / Gospel Arts Website

Case study

The artist imagines it. I make it work.

An artist paints a scripture every week, and preachers pass each painting on over WeChat to readers across Southeast Asia and North America. They came with a list of wishes for a website and no idea what a professional site has to consider. My part was to turn those wishes into a system, and to keep it running well after each request was done.

Asking what they didn't know to ask

The first brief was a two-year plan in a few lines: a home page, browsing by theme, managing the artwork, news of exhibitions, a way to give feedback, a copyright notice, and later a shop and a team. I answered with a checklist of the questions each line hides. Who may upload, and does anything need review before it goes public? May visitors share or change what they download? Which languages come first? Who signs in, and how?

The paintings themselves were only image files, with no dates, scripture or any other text attached. Browsing by theme or by date needs that information, so I asked the artist to gather it first. They collected it into a spreadsheet and kept it in Dropbox with the images.

Other answers reshaped the plan. Asked how many subscribers to expect, the artist checked with the people who forward the paintings and came back with a much smaller, more realistic estimate. Later the artist learned that most readers are older people who only use a phone, opening links inside WeChat. From then on I designed the site for phones first.

A look made for these paintings

The site's visual design is custom, drawn from the paintings the artist provided and from inspiration in everyday life, rather than from a template. The first sketch was a gallery page in deep blue-black and gold, and the artist said the colors and style were exactly what they loved. That direction grew into the stained-glass look the site has today, built in code and refined on live previews rather than in static mockups.

Choosing tools a non-technical team can live with

I compared ways to manage the content and proposed the one that was easiest for people who aren't developers, cheapest to run, and still met every need. The artist's spreadsheet and Dropbox folder fed an early prototype, enough to design the pages and test how the paintings looked. Dropbox limits API access and wasn't stable enough to serve a live site, so it stayed in that stage, and the archive, hundreds of images and over 300 MB, moved into a content studio.

Turning a request into a workflow

Most requests arrive as a one-time task. I treat each as the first run of something that will happen again, and design for the next time.

"I'd like to upload my own work."

A complete artwork management flow in the studio: drafts, previews, bulk changes with Undo, a scripture picker that catches typos, plus written guides for the team.

"Put a watermark on every painting."

A watermark step built into every future upload, with defaults the artist can adjust per piece. Originals stay private, outside the public site.

Rolling out

"Readers should be able to share what moved them."

Comments and sign-in designed together with staff roles, a review queue and a moderation word list, so the team can look after the space.

Designed

"Can it reach people in three languages?"

Content is written once in Traditional Chinese, converted to Simplified and proofread, and paired with English, with scripture fetched in each language.

Working with a non-technical client

  • I set the agenda. I wrote the project's working documents, from the collaboration checklist and content list to how the site will be run and the rules that go with it, so the artist always knew what was decided, what was waiting, and what was theirs to provide.
  • No blank pages. Every open decision reaches the artist with a draft, a length limit from the layout, and a few options with my recommendation.
  • Big ideas get a reality check. When the artist suggested inviting other artists to create together, I laid out what it would take to make it work. They saw it needed more thought and moved it to a later phase themselves.
  • Plans before code. Bigger steps, like accounts and a shop, start as written plans the artist approves before anything is built.
  • Real sites, not mockups. Each iteration goes up at its own preview address before it reaches the live site, and the full test suite runs there, not on the live site.

Stack: Next.js, TypeScript, Sanity, Vercel, Cloudflare (DNS, Email Routing, R2), Playwright.
